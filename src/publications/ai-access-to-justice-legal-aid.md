## Introduction

The Justice Committee’s inquiry places AI in legal aid against two linked questions: how operating pressures affect access to justice, and whether digital innovation can support it.[^1] For legal-aid users in England and Wales, access means more than receiving legal information; it means being able to enforce rights, resolve disputes and obtain effective advice, mediation or representation.[^2]

This essay argues that AI is a conditional net positive. It can increase access in two ways: by giving legal-aid lawyers more time for clients, and by helping people reach supervised triage earlier. Conversely, it can undermine access if AI output is treated as legal advice, because error is then shifted onto people least able to detect it. The answer is therefore not to reject AI, but to control where it sits in the legal-aid process: it should reduce routine work and speed up entry to help, while lawyers remain responsible for the decisions that determine whether a person gets advice and representation.

## Provider Capacity

AI helps legal aid most when it gives lawyers more time for clients, not when it replaces legal judgment. The capacity problem is concrete: Ministry of Justice research records a 41% decline in civil legal aid provider numbers since 2011–12, while half of surveyed providers reported “very high or overwhelming” demand.[^3] Provider evidence confirms that demand often exceeds supply, workforce concerns are acute, and clients frequently need emotional support that fixed-fee structures do not recognise.[^4]

AI could therefore increase access by reducing the time lawyers spend on bounded, lawyer-reviewed tasks. Saved routine time matters because the evidence shows a shortage of professional time, not merely a shortage of legal information. AI cannot repair that capacity crisis on its own; it improves access only when the time it releases is used for advice, representation and client support. If the saving is absorbed as cost control, AI has not widened access: it has made an overstretched service cheaper to run.

## Early Triage

AI could also help people reach legal aid earlier, before problems escalate. That matters because LASPO removed early legal advice from areas including housing, family and welfare, while Ministry of Justice evidence recognises that early intervention can prevent escalation and support broader access to justice.[^5]

Supervised AI could therefore help people with unresolved legal problems by helping them describe their issue, check basic eligibility and reach an adviser sooner. Steenhuis and Westermann’s legal aid intake pilot illustrates this kind of supervised triage: it used hand-coded rules and an LLM to indicate likely eligibility before an applicant waited in a telephone queue, could ask follow-up questions, and expressly warned that AI “can get the decision wrong”.[^6]

Crucially, the authors framed the task as minimum intake screening, “not provide legal advice”.[^7] Hagan supports the same boundary: AI may orient users but not give a definitive answer.[^8] The US-based pilot therefore supports only a narrow point: supervised intake can help people enter legal aid without replacing advice, provided users can still reach a human adviser.

## Substitution and Error

For legal-aid users, the strongest objection to AI is not that it is imperfect, but that imperfection may be imposed on those least able to detect it. Magesh *et al* found that commercial legal research AI tools still hallucinated: over one in six Lexis+ AI and Ask Practical Law AI queries produced misleading or false information, and one-third of Westlaw responses contained a hallucination.[^9] Those errors make substitution especially dangerous for people who rely on legal aid. Even specialist legal AI can produce false legal information; a person without legal expertise may not know whether an answer is current, binding or jurisdictionally relevant. If that output is treated as legal help, the system has not widened justice but transferred the cost of error to the person least able to bear it.[^10]

*Ayinde* shows the consequence when such material reaches court: unchecked generative AI produced false legal information, typically fake citations or quotations. The court warned that misuse has serious implications for the administration of justice and public confidence, since it can distort proceedings and make legal outcomes appear unreliable.[^11]

This risk falls hardest where people lack practical help to use online legal systems. Denvir and Selvarajah show that online justice requires support with digital systems and non-digital routes, while Ofcom records that digital exclusion depends on skills, confidence, device quality and affordability, not just internet access.[^12] For legal-aid users, the point is practical: an AI-first route may be hardest to use safely for those already most dependent on legal aid. The strongest objection is that imperfect AI may still be better than no advice. “No advice” is a policy failure, not the benchmark for justice; the fair comparator is a hybrid legal-aid service, not abandonment.

## The Hybrid Model

The real transformation for legal-aid users is a hybrid route to help, not AI replacing advice. A rights-preserving system would draw a sharp line between support and substitution. Low-risk administration can proceed where AI summarises papers, organises evidence or prepares information for review, because those tasks leave advisers responsible; judicial guidance accepts summarisation as useful but insists accuracy is checked.[^13]

High-risk uses should be rejected or tightly controlled: legal research that cannot be independently verified, legal analysis, court-facing material and automated eligibility decisions with serious consequences. The ICO’s guidance supports that boundary through fairness, accuracy, non-discrimination, data-protection impact assessments and safeguards for automated decisions with legal or similarly significant effects.[^14] On that model, AI transforms legal aid by widening the route to help while keeping human responsibility for judgment, advice and remedy.

## Conclusion

AI is therefore a conditional net positive for legal-aid users, not because it makes law cheaper, but because it can redirect scarce professional attention towards those who need it. The decisive question is who gains from the efficiency and who bears the mistakes. If legal-aid users receive earlier, checked and accountable help, AI can widen access to justice. If they receive automated answers instead of funded advice, it only makes exclusion faster and less visible.
